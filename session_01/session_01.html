Forecasting Problems and Data Structures

Why Forecast?

Forecasting plays a central role in economic and financial decision-making. Examples from this course:

Stock returns

Which stocks are likely to perform better? Implications for portfolio allocation

Macroeconomics

How will inflation and economic activity evolve? Implications for economic policy

Volatility

How risky will financial markets be? Implications for risk management

Energy markets

What will electricity or oil prices be? Implications for trading and planning

The forecasting objective differs considerably across these applications

The Forecasting Problem

At time \(t\), we want to forecast an outcome \(Y_{t+h}\), where \(h\) is the forecast horizon. We have an information set \(\mathcal{I}_t\) containing information available at time \(t\). A forecasting method produces

\[\widehat{Y}_{t+h|t} = f_t(\mathcal{I}_t)\]

Target

What do we forecast?

Horizon

How far ahead?

Information

What is known when the forecast is made?

A genuine forecast cannot use information that becomes available after the forecast origin

What Exactly Are We Forecasting?

Application Typical target Example
Stock returns Future return Next month’s stock return
Macroeconomics Growth rate or change Quarterly inflation or GDP growth
Volatility Future variance or volatility Next day’s realized variance
Energy Price level or return Tomorrow’s hourly electricity prices; future crude oil returns
  • Forecasting a price level is different from forecasting its return or change
  • Forecasting volatility is different from forecasting the direction of returns

The definition of the target influences the choice of models, predictors, and evaluation

Time-Series, Cross-Sectional and Panel Forecasting

Time-series forecasting

Predict future observations of a variable using historical information

Example: US inflation over time

Cross-sectional prediction

Predict differences across individual units

Example: which stocks are expected to outperform others next month?

Panel forecasting

Observations for many units over time: \(Y_{i,t}\), \(i = 1, \ldots, N\), \(t = 1, \ldots, T\)

Example: monthly returns for thousands of individual stocks

Important distinction: Panel forecasting can involve both time-series prediction and cross-sectional comparisons

Example: Predicting Individual Stock Returns

Suppose we observe monthly data for a large number of stocks. For each stock \(i\) at time \(t\), the objective is to predict next month’s return,

\[\widehat{r}_{i,t+1|t} = f(X_{i,t})\]

where \(X_{i,t}\) contains predictors available for stock \(i\) at time \(t\).

Stock Month Predictors Target
A January \(X_{A,\text{Jan}}\) \(r_{A,\text{Feb}}\)
B January \(X_{B,\text{Jan}}\) \(r_{B,\text{Feb}}\)
A February \(X_{A,\text{Feb}}\) \(r_{A,\text{Mar}}\)
B February \(X_{B,\text{Feb}}\) \(r_{B,\text{Mar}}\)

Example: Predicting Individual Stock Returns II

  • The model uses information across both stocks and historical months
  • Predictions can be evaluated for individual stocks or used to rank stocks for portfolio construction

Different Forecasting Problems, Different Challenges

Stock returns

Returns contain substantial noise; predictive signals are often weak

Inflation

Inflation can be persistent, but predictive relationships may change across economic conditions

Volatility

Volatility exhibits strong persistence and clustering over time

Electricity prices

Strong daily and weekly patterns, together with occasional extreme price movements

Different forecasting problems have different data structures and sources of predictability.

No single forecasting method is necessarily best across all applications

Forecasting Information and Predictors

Where Does Predictive Information Come From?

A forecasting model uses observable information to predict an unknown future outcome,

\[\widehat{Y}_{t+h|t} = f(X_t)\]

where \(X_t\) denotes the available predictors.

Where Does Predictive Information Come From? II

Predictive information may come from:

Past values of the target Lagged inflation, volatility, or electricity prices

Other economic variables Interest rates, employment, economic activity, and firm characteristics

Constructed features Moving averages, momentum measures, technical indicators, and interactions

Alternative data Internet searches, news, or financial text

The usefulness of each information source depends on the forecasting problem

Persistence and Lagged Predictors

Many economic and financial variables exhibit persistence: past values contain information about future outcomes. A simple autoregressive forecasting model is

\[Y_{t+1} = \alpha + \sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\beta_j Y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_{t+1}\]

Machine learning can use the same lagged inputs in a more flexible forecasting function,

\[\widehat{Y}_{t+1|t} = f(Y_t, Y_{t-1}, \ldots, Y_{t-p+1}, Z_t)\]

Persistence and Lagged Predictors II

Examples:

Inflation

Past inflation and other macroeconomic indicators

Volatility

Recent realized volatility

Electricity prices

Prices from previous hours and days

The relevance of lags depends on the variable, the horizon, and the stability of the underlying relationships

Example: Volatility Persistence

  • Financial market volatility exhibits substantial persistence: high-volatility periods often follow other high-volatility periods
  • The HAR model uses realized volatility measured over different historical horizons
  • Let \(RV_t\) denote daily realized variance. Construct weekly and monthly averages,

\[RV_t^{(w)} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{j=0}^{4} RV_{t-j}, \qquad RV_t^{(m)} = \frac{1}{22}\sum_{j=0}^{21} RV_{t-j}\]

Example: The HAR Model

A simple HAR forecasting specification is

\[RV_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_d RV_t + \beta_w RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m RV_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+1}\]

ML models can use these predictors while allowing nonlinear relationships and interactions

Application: Christensen et al. (2023) compare ML with HAR-type models

Example: Firm Characteristics and Stock Returns

In stock return prediction, explanatory variables often differ across firms as well as over time. Common firm characteristics include:

Size

Market capitalization

Valuation

Book-to-market and related ratios

Momentum

Past stock returns over selected horizons

Risk/trading activity

Volatility, turnover, liquidity measures

Example: Firm Characteristics and Stock Returns II

The forecasting model combines these variables,

\[\widehat{r}_{i,t+1|t} = f(\text{Size}_{i,t}, \text{Momentum}_{i,t}, \text{Volatility}_{i,t}, \ldots)\]

Predictors can also interact with aggregate economic and market conditions

Application: Gu et al. (2020) examine a large collection of firm characteristics and macroeconomic interactions

Predictors Observed at Different Frequencies

Tasks often involve information observed at different frequencies. Examples:

  • Daily financial market data
  • Weekly unemployment claims
  • Monthly inflation and employment indicators
  • Quarterly GDP

Mixed-frequency forecasting seeks to exploit information without necessarily reducing all predictors to the lowest frequency

Mixed Frequencies and Nowcasting

  • Nowcasting: estimating a current or very recent economic outcome before its official value becomes available
  • Example: Borup et al. (2023) use daily internet search data to nowcast and backcast weekly US initial unemployment claims

Important: The date an observation refers to and the date it becomes available are not necessarily the same

Feature Construction: Turning Data into Predictors

Raw observations can be transformed into predictors that summarize relevant patterns. Common feature constructions:

  • Lags: \(Y_t, Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2}, \ldots\)
  • Moving averages:

\[Y_t^{(k)} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} Y_{t-j}\]

  • Changes and returns: differences or percentage changes instead of levels
  • Calendar variables: hour of day, day of week, or season

Feature Construction II

Examples from energy forecasting:

Electricity

  • Lagged prices
  • Demand forecasts
  • Calendar effects

Crude oil

  • Macroeconomic variables
  • Technical indicators

Features must be constructed using information available at the forecast origin

Forecasting Objectives and Machine Learning

Forecast Errors and Loss Functions

A forecasting method produces a prediction \(\widehat{Y}_{t+h|t}\) of the future outcome \(Y_{t+h}\). Once the outcome is observed, the forecast error is

\[e_{t+h|t} = Y_{t+h} - \widehat{Y}_{t+h|t}\]

Forecasting methods aim to minimize some measure of forecast error, represented by a loss function,

\[L(Y_{t+h}, \widehat{Y}_{t+h|t}) = L(e_{t+h|t})\]

Different loss functions penalize forecasting mistakes differently.

The choice of loss function determines what the forecasting model seeks to predict

Squared and Absolute Error Loss

Two commonly used loss functions are:

Squared error loss

\[L(e) = e^2\]

Large errors receive disproportionately greater penalties

Absolute error loss

\[L(e) = |e|\]

Loss increases linearly with the magnitude of the error

For example, increasing the absolute error from 1 to 3:

  • Squared loss increases from 1 to 9
  • Absolute loss increases from 1 to 3

Squared loss is more sensitive to extreme forecast errors than absolute loss

Optimal Forecasts under Squared Loss

Suppose we want to forecast \(Y_{t+h}\) given information \(\mathcal{I}_t\). Under squared loss, the optimal forecast \(f_t^{*}\) solves

\[\min_{f}\, \mathrm{E}\left[(Y_{t+h} - f)^2 \mid \mathcal{I}_t\right]\]

The first-order condition is

\[\mathrm{E}\left[Y_{t+h} - f_t^{*} \mid \mathcal{I}_t\right] = 0\]

Optimal Forecasts under Squared Loss II

Hence,

\[f_t^{*} = \mathrm{E}\left[Y_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{I}_t\right]\]

  • Under squared loss, the optimal point forecast is the conditional mean

Implication: Regression-based ML methods trained using squared loss aim to approximate the conditional mean

Different Loss Functions, Different Forecasts

The optimal forecast depends on the loss function, not only on the available data.

Loss function Optimal point forecast
Squared error Conditional mean
Absolute error Conditional median
Asymmetric absolute error Conditional quantile
  • Example: forecasting electricity prices in the presence of occasional extreme spikes
  • Conditional means and medians may differ substantially when the predictive distribution is asymmetric or contains extreme outcomes
  • Most standard regression-based ML applications use squared loss, but alternative objectives can be used when appropriate

From Linear Forecasting to Machine Learning

A traditional linear forecasting model:

\[Y_{t+1} = \alpha + \beta' X_t + \varepsilon_{t+1}\]

The forecast is a linear function of the predictors:

\[\widehat{Y}_{t+1|t} = \widehat{\alpha} + \widehat{\beta}' X_t\]

From Linear Forecasting to Machine Learning II

Machine learning allows more flexible forecasting functions,

\[\widehat{Y}_{t+1|t} = \widehat{f}(X_t)\]

Ridge and LASSO

Regularized linear models

Random forests

Nonlinearities and interactions

Neural networks

Flexible nonlinear functions

These methods can share the same forecasting objective while differing in how they approximate the predictive relationship

Model Flexibility and Overfitting

Flexible models can capture complex predictive relationships, but may also fit noise in the data. Two familiar problems:

Underfitting

  • A model is too restrictive to capture relevant predictive patterns

Overfitting

  • A model fits the training observations well but generalizes poorly to new observations

The bias–variance trade-off illustrates the tension between model flexibility and estimation uncertainty

Model Flexibility and Overfitting II

ML methods control complexity through:

  • Regularization in linear models
  • Restrictions on tree complexity
  • Regularization and early stopping in neural networks

Central question: when does additional model flexibility capture genuine predictive information rather than noise?

Why Does ML Work? Variable Selection and Interpretation

Where Do ML Forecasting Gains Come From?

Machine learning may improve forecasting through several distinct mechanisms:

1

More predictive information

Incorporating additional variables and constructed features

2

Regularization and variable selection

Controlling estimation uncertainty when many predictors are available

3

Nonlinearities and interactions

Capturing predictive relationships that linear models may miss

4

Model selection and tuning

Choosing specifications and hyperparameters based on predictive performance

These mechanisms are related, but conceptually different

Research connection: Goulet Coulombe et al. (2022) investigate why ML improves macroeconomic forecasting, emphasizing nonlinearities, regularization, cross-validation, and loss functions

Many Predictors, But Which Ones Matter?

Modern applications often contain large collections of predictors. Examples:

  • Stock returns: firm characteristics and macroeconomic variables
  • Inflation: a large set of macroeconomic and financial indicators
  • Oil returns: macroeconomic variables and technical indicators

Many Predictors, But Which Ones Matter? II

Including more predictors does not necessarily improve forecasting:

  • Some predictors contain little information
  • Many predictors are strongly correlated
  • Estimation becomes less precise as model complexity increases

Many Predictors, But Which Ones Matter? III

A central question is whether we can identify a relatively small set of useful predictors, or exploit many predictors without overfitting

Applications: Medeiros et al. (2021) study inflation forecasting with many predictors, while Zhang et al. (2019) examine LASSO-based predictor selection for crude oil returns

Variable Selection and Regularization

Consider a linear forecasting model with \(p\) predictors, \(Y_{t+1} = \alpha + X_t'\beta + \varepsilon_{t+1}\). LASSO estimates coefficients by solving

\[\min_{\alpha,\beta}\left\{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(Y_{t+1} - \alpha - X_t'\beta\right)^2 + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_j|\right\}\]

The penalty shrinks coefficients and can set some exactly to zero. Two potential benefits:

  • Reducing estimation uncertainty
  • Identifying a smaller set of predictors

Variable Selection and Regularization II

  • However, selection need not be stable: correlated predictors may substitute for one another

Important distinction: Selecting a variable because it improves prediction does not establish that the variable has a causal effect on the outcome

Nonlinearities and Interactions

Linear forecasting models impose an additive relationship with constant coefficients,

\[\widehat{Y}_{t+1|t} = \alpha + \beta_1 X_{1,t} + \beta_2 X_{2,t}\]

Predictive relationships may instead involve nonlinearities and interactions,

\[\widehat{Y}_{t+1|t} = \alpha + \beta_1 X_{1,t} + \beta_2 X_{2,t} + \gamma X_{1,t}X_{2,t}\]

The predictive effect of \(X_{1,t}\) now depends on the value of \(X_{2,t}\),

\[\frac{\partial \widehat{Y}_{t+1|t}}{\partial X_{1,t}} = \beta_1 + \gamma X_{2,t}\]

Nonlinearities and Interactions II

Examples:

  • Relevance of financial predictor may depend on market conditions
  • Macroeconomic links may differ between expansions and recessions

Trees and neural networks can learn such relationships without specifying each interaction explicitly

How Can We Identify the Source of Forecasting Gains?

Suppose a flexible ML model outperforms a traditional forecasting regression. Is the improvement due to:

  • Better predictors?
  • A more flexible functional form?
  • Regularization or model tuning?

How Can We Identify the Source of Forecasting Gains? II

Controlled comparisons can help distinguish these possibilities:

Comparison Predictors Main question
OLS vs. LASSO Same Does regularization help?
Linear vs. nonlinear ML Same Does flexibility improve prediction?
ML with vs. without new features Different Do new predictors add information?

These help explain why one approach performs better, rather than merely documenting which model has the lowest forecast error

Variable Selection, Importance, and Interpretation

Three related but different questions:

Concept Main question
Variable selection Which predictors should enter the model?
Variable importance Which predictors contribute most to the fitted model’s predictions or predictive performance?
Model interpretation How do the model’s predictions change with particular predictor values?

Variable Selection, Importance, and Interpretation II

Examples of tools:

  • LASSO for variable selection
  • Permutation-based variable importance
  • Partial dependence and accumulated local effects for interpretation

Importance and interpretation depend on the fitted model and the data distribution

Predictive importance does not imply economic/causal importance

Application Relevant questions Research example
Stock returns Which characteristics help predict returns? Are nonlinear interactions important? Gu et al. (2020)
Inflation Which macroeconomic indicators are informative? How nonlinear are the relationships? Medeiros et al. (2021)

What Do Forecasting Models Learn? II

  • A forecasting model can be useful even when its predictions are difficult to interpret
  • However, understanding its predictive structure can help assess robustness, improve models, and generate new economic hypotheses

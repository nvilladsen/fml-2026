Research question: Can machine learning methods improve forecasting of asset returns?

Empirical Asset Pricing

  • Asset pricing asks why some assets earn higher expected returns than others, and why expected returns change over time
  • The risk premium is the expected return in excess of the risk-free rate, the compensation for bearing risk
  • It is unobserved. We observe the realized returns, which are dominated by unforecastable news
  • Measuring the risk premium is therefore a forecasting problem, e.g., forecast the excess returns of next month using information available today

Many correlated predictors and an unknown functional form make this a natural application for machine learning

Functional form is the mathematical shape of the relation between the predictors and the expected return.

Forecasting Target (Sec. 1)

  • The authors aim to forecast the excess returns of an asset
  • Construct an “additive prediction-error model”,

\[r_{i,t+1} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_t[r_{i,t+1}]}_{g^\ast(z_{i,t})} + \varepsilon_{i,t+1}\]

  • \(r_{i,t+1}\) is the excess return (over the T-bill rate) of stock \(i\) at time \(t+1\)
  • \(g^\ast(\cdot)\) is the forecasting function, the conditional expected return (the risk premium)
  • \(z_{i,t}\) is the information set (predictors) for stock \(i\) at time \(t\)
  • \(\varepsilon_{i,t+1}\) is the error term

The T-bill rate proxies the risk-free rate. \(g^\ast(\cdot)\) is the unknown true conditional expectation (on \(z\) our information set) and \(g(z;\theta)\) is our attempt to approximate it with a chosen functional form.

An additive-error model says the outcome splits into two parts that are simply added: a part the information set can predict, and a part it cannot.

Conditioning on the information set, in terms of the conditional expected return.

Predictors (Sec. 2.1)

Stock characteristics

94 characteristics \(c_{i,t}\) from the cross-section literature

Macro variables

8 variables \(x_t\) from Goyal and Welch (2008): dp, ep, bm, ntis, tbl, tms, dfy, svar

Industry

74 dummy variables, one per two-digit SIC industry code

Stock characteristics are made to interact with the macro variables,

\[z_{i,t} = (1, x_t^\intercal)^\intercal \otimes c_{i,t}, \qquad p = 94\times(8+1)+74 = 920\]

Here \(p\) is the number of predictors

The symbol \(\otimes\) multiplies each entry of the first list by the whole second list and stacks the results into one longer list, e.g. \((1, x) \otimes (c_1,c_2) = (c_1, c_2, x\cdot c_1, x\cdot c_2)\), and \((2, 3) \otimes (5, 7) = (2 \cdot 5, 2 \cdot 7, 3 \cdot 5, 3 \cdot 7) = (10, 14, 15, 21)\). In the slide, the leading 1 keeps each characteristic on its own while x pairs it with the macro variable, giving 94 × (8 + 1) = 846 terms before adding the 74 industry dummies to reach p = 920.

dp: dividend-price ratio, ep: earnings-price ratio, bm: book-to-market ratio, ntis: net equity expansion, tbl: Treasury-bill rate, tms: term spread, dfy: default spread, svar: stock variance

Stock characteristics are firm-level variables observed for each stock and month, such as size, book-to-market and momentum. They are closely related to anomalies but not the same thing. An anomaly is a documented pattern in average returns that standard asset pricing models do not explain. Many of the characteristics were first documented as anomalies, and the paper builds them “based on the cross-section of stock returns literature”. The characteristic is the variable and the anomaly is the return pattern it is linked to, so they are NOT anomalies per se.

SIC code: standard industrial classification, four digit code, a firm’s main line of business. The first two digits give the broad industry. For example, 28 is chemicals.

The Paper in Numbers

920 baseline predictors per stock and month

~30,000 stocks monthly returns, 1957–2016

−3.46% vs. 0.40% monthly \(R^2_{\text{oos}}\): OLS with all predictors vs. the best trees and neural networks

Forecasting excess returns is a low signal-to-noise problem: a monthly \(R^2_{\text{oos}}\) of a fraction of a percent can be economically meaningful (Campbell and Thompson, 2008)

The \(R^2_{\text{OOS}}\) is benchmarked against a 0-valued forecast.

The Authors’ Main Findings

Linear models break down with many predictors

OLS with all 920 predictors: \(R^2_{\text{oos}} = -3.46\%\) per month, worse than forecasting zero excess returns

Regularization repairs the linear model

Elastic net, PCR and PLS turn the \(R^2_{\text{oos}}\) positive (0.11% to 0.27%)

Non-linearity helps, via interactions

Trees and neural networks reach 0.33% to 0.40% (splines without interactions (GLM) do not surpass the best linear models)

Moderate depth networks win

Neural network performance peaks at three hidden layers, outperforming all machine learning techniques

\[ \vdots \]

Training: 1957–2003

Validation: 2004–2015

  • The training sample is an expanding window, one more year at every refit
  • The validation sample is a rolling window of fixed length, 12 years
  • The models are refit once a year, due to computational overhead and low frequency of signal (data/predictor) arrival: 30 estimations instead of 360 over 30 test years
  • Each fitted model is tested on the next year only

Hyperparameters and Validation (Sec. 1.1)

The regularization approaches of the paper rely on hyperparameters, e.g.,

  • The penalty parameter (elastic net)
  • The number of components (PLS, PCR)
  • The tree depth (random forests, boosted trees)

They control model complexity, and there is little theoretical guidance on how to set them

Training model on the training sample for given hyperparameters

Validation evaluate on the validation sample, compute the loss

Model Selection select hyperparameters with the best validation loss

Bias, Variance, and Regularization (Sec. 1.3)

Flexible models

  • A flexible model (nonlinearities, interactions, deep trees, neural networks) can approximate a wide range of functions, so bias is low
  • But if it is too flexible for the data, it has many parameters to estimate from limited information, so the estimates have high variance and are unstable
  • It then fits noise (adapts to the current sample) rather than signal and performs poorly out of sample

Regularized models

  • Accept some bias in return for lower variance
  • By means of penalties (LASSO, ridge, elastic net), fewer dimensions (PCR and PLS), shallow trees, early stopping, or averaging over trees or seeds
  • The degree of regularization is a hyperparameter and can be determined via validation

Evaluation: Out-of-Sample \(R^2\) (Sec. 1.8)

Forecasts are evaluated on the test sample (out-of-sample), pooling all stocks and months,

\[R^2_{\text{oos}} = 1 - \frac{\sum (r_{i,t+1} - \hat{r}_{i,t+1})^2}{\sum r_{i,t+1}^2}\]

  • Compares the squared forecast errors of the model with those of a forecast of zero
  • A positive \(R^2_{\text{oos}}\) means that the model beats a zero-excess-returns forecast (difficult!)

The benchmark must be motivated and specified by the researcher

\[R^2_{\text{oos}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t+1}} (r_{i,t+1} - \hat{r}_{i,t+1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t+1}} r_{i,t+1}^2}\]

The Models

OLS+H

Linear regression on all 920 predictors

OLS-3+H

Linear regression on size, book-to-market and momentum only

Elastic net+H

Penalized linear regression with both \(\ell_1\) and \(\ell_2\) penalties

PCR

Principal Components Regression on \(K\) principal components, the main directions of variation in the predictors

PLS

Partial Least Squares Regression on \(K\) components built to covary with the returns (target), not just with the predictors

GLM+H

Generalized Linear Model: Linear regression on piecewise-polynomial (spline) transformations of each predictor, no interactions

Trees

Tree-based models: Random forests average many deep trees, boosted trees (+H) sequentially add many shallow trees

Neural networks

Feed-forward neural networks, NN1 to NN5 models have one to five hidden layers

+H marks models estimated with the Huber loss: squared error for small forecast errors and absolute error for large ones

Same order of magnitude

  • OLS-3: 0.20% vs. 0.16%
  • PCR: 0.29% vs. 0.26%
  • Elastic net: 0.17% vs. 0.11%
  • The pattern: OLS-3 and the regularized linear models are positive, the full OLS is not

Does not reproduce

  • OLS: −141.38% vs. −3.46%
  • PLS: 0.18% vs. 0.27% (All) and −0.62% vs. −0.14% (Top 1,000)
  • Stocks per month: 5,225 vs. more than 6,200

Explaining Why

Why Does OLS Do Worse Than the Other Models?

Two sources of forecast error: bias (the model systematically misses part of the true relation) and variance (the estimates are unstable across samples),

OLS

920 coefficients

Low bias, but high variance: with noisy returns the coefficients are poorly determined, so the forecasts are unstable too. Paper: \(R^2_{\text{oos}} = -3.46\%\)

OLS-3

3 coefficients

Low variance, but the other 917 predictors are ignored, so the bias is higher. Paper: \(R^2_{\text{oos}} = 0.16\%\)

Regularized models

Shrinkage or compression

Use the predictors, but shrink the coefficients or compress them into a few components: a little bias for much lower variance. Paper: \(R^2_{\text{oos}} = 0.11\%\) to \(0.27\%\)

Why Does Our OLS Differ From the Paper’s?

Our OLS gives \(-141.38\%\), the paper’s \(-3.46\%\). Three differences between our implementation and theirs could explain this,

Difference Possible mechanism Status
Solver for the Huber loss (start, step size, stopping rule) Stopping early shrinks the coefficients Matches All once calibrated
Predictor construction (scaling, missing values) Changes which columns are extreme or near-constant (potential source of instability) Not tested
Data Different sample of stocks: 5,225 vs. more than 6,200 per month Not tested

We matched the paper’s number by calibrating the solver, but a match does not prove the cause

Theory: The Variance of the OLS Estimator

\[y = X\beta + \varepsilon, \qquad \hat\beta = (X^\intercal X)^{-1}X^\intercal y, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta \mid X) = \sigma^2 (X^\intercal X)^{-1}\]

where the errors \(\varepsilon\) have variance \(\sigma^2\)

  • If a column of \(X\) is almost all zeros, or almost a copy of other columns, the data say very little about its coefficient
  • The variance of that coefficient estimate is then very large

Little variation in a predictor and high correlation between predictors have the same effect: the coefficients are poorly determined

Theory: What Regularization Does

Ridge regression penalizes the size of the coefficients,

\[\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}} =(X^\intercal X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\intercal y\]

  • All coefficients are shrunk towards zero with a strength governed by \(\lambda\) (\(\lambda = 0\) \(\to\) OLS)
  • Adding \(\lambda I\) to \(X^\intercal X\) makes the estimates less sensitive to the data, which lowers the variance at the cost of bias
  • Early stopping has a similar effect: gradient descent starts with all coefficients at zero and moves towards the OLS solution, so stopping early leaves them closer to zero
